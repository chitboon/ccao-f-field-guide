# The Strengthening Sprint

**Five days.** This is not a course. It is what you do *after* the courses, to convert familiarity into a pass.

---

## Who this is for

You have skimmed the Anthropic Academy material — *AI Fluency*, *Claude 101*, and ideally *AI Capabilities and Limitations* — as the exam guide recommends. You use Claude at work. You broadly recognise Projects, artifacts, Research, the 4Ds.

You are not starting from zero, and this plan does not treat you as though you are. It assumes the reading is done and the problem is **strengthening**: finding the gaps that familiarity hides, and fixing the procedural leaks that cost prepared candidates marks.

If you have *not* skimmed the courses, do that first. It is roughly twelve hours and this plan is far less effective without it. The links are in the [README](README.md).

---

## How this plan was derived

It is reverse-engineered from one candidate's actual preparation — a passing sitting, prepared in a few days, with a full record of what was done and what it produced. Not from a theory of how studying ought to work.

**One caveat, stated plainly.** That candidate had already completed preparation for a harder credential in the same family, arriving with a verified blueprint and purpose-built drill material. The few days were spent on the *delta*, not on the subject. If you are coming to this cold, the reading above is not optional and five days is optimistic.

---

## The six moves that did the work

Before the day-by-day, these are the principles. If you only take one thing, take the first.

### 1 · Diff the blueprint. Don't study it.

The single highest-leverage move. Go through the seven domains and give each an honest verdict: *already solid*, *needs reframing*, *genuine gap*. Then study only the gaps.

Most candidates study everything evenly, which spends the most time on what they already know. The exam grades **per objective**, so an hour spent on an objective you have already mastered buys nothing at all.

### 2 · Test first, read second

Do not read a domain and then test yourself on it. Sit the questions cold, then read only what the misses point at.

This inverts the usual instinct and it is more efficient by a wide margin. Reading an explanation you would have got right feels productive and teaches nothing. The test is not the assessment — **the test is the diagnostic.**

### 3 · Many small sittings, one full-length

Sets of ten and twenty with immediate per-item feedback, and exactly one sealed full-length paper near the end.

The small sets build judgment, because feedback arrives while the reasoning is still in your head. The full-length paper measures something different — whether that judgment holds across 120 minutes with no feedback at all. Both are needed and they are not substitutes.

### 4 · Log the *kind* of miss, not the count

Three buckets, every time:

| Bucket | What it means | What fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Didn't know it** | Genuine knowledge gap | Read the source lesson |
| **Misread the item** | Missed a count, a qualifier, a "FIRST" | Pacing discipline, not study |
| **Chose the soft option** | Took the plausible over the structural | The highest-value fix available |

**The distribution is the diagnostic.** In the preparation this plan is drawn from, essentially every miss fell into buckets two and three — which meant the right response was to stop studying content and start drilling procedure. Without the bucket log, that would have looked like a knowledge problem and been treated as one, wastefully.

### 5 · Treat a procedural leak as a leak

Multi-response undercounting — answering a Select TWO with one letter — cost that candidate three items across the week. The fix was not reading about multi-response items. It was a drill set where **every** item was multi-response with varying counts, until counting before submitting became automatic.

Result: seven for seven on the full-length paper, including a Select THREE. A procedural leak is worth finding early precisely because it is so cheap to close.

### 6 · Make the last session entirely your own misses

Not a fresh mock. Not a re-read. A short set built only from the traps your log says catch you.

That was the highest-value hour of the entire week, and it is only available if you have been keeping the log from day one.

## Day by day

### Day 1 · Diff and diagnose (2 hours)

Read the blueprint's seven domains and 27-odd objectives properly — this is the one reading task that cannot be skipped, because it defines the target.

Then give each domain a verdict. Be honest; optimism here costs you later.

Then sit **[practice/frameworks.md](practice/frameworks.md)** cold — items 1–8 at minimum. These test the four Ds at the level candidates report being asked about them. Log misses by bucket.

**Expect to be surprised.** Framework recall is the most commonly under-prepared area, because it lives in the courses rather than in the blueprint.

### Day 2 · The gaps, and the item type nobody expects (2–3 hours)

Work only the domains you marked as gaps. For most people that is Product and Model Selection, Configuration and Knowledge Management, and Governance — the blueprint's D3, D5 and D6.

Then sit **[practice/sequencing.md](practice/sequencing.md)**. Give this real attention: five-activity ordering items can account for a meaningful share of the paper, they are almost absent from other prep material, and candidates consistently report them as the biggest time sink.

Learn the three-question method — *which mode, which framework, then slots* — rather than memorising sequences.

### Day 3 · Judgment, and the procedural leak (2–3 hours)

The heaviest domain is Output Evaluation and Validation, and Anthropic's own research says why it needs deliberate work: discernment neither accumulates with time served nor transfers from knowing the product well. Knowing the product well does not make you better at judging its output.

Work the failure taxonomy until you can name a failure type from a description: hallucination, bias, incompleteness, inconsistency, sycophancy, silent truncation, letter-over-spirit.

Then drill **every multi-response item** you can find, counts varying. Not for the content — for the habit. Count your letters against the stated number before moving on, every single time.

### Day 4 · The full-length paper (2h 30m)

Sit **[practice/mock-exam-60.md](practice/mock-exam-60.md)** in one session, 120 minutes, sealed, no lookups.

Score it. **Then stop** — do not review yet. Sort the misses into buckets first. The distribution tells you what tomorrow is for; reading rationales immediately tells you nothing except which answers were right.

Read the [key's caveats](practice/mock-exam-60-key.md) before you interpret the score.

### Day 5 · Your own misses, then taper (1–2 hours)

Build or request a short set from your bucket-three misses only — the plausible-but-soft ones. That is where the remaining marks are.

Then the cram material, and stop. Nothing new on the final day. In the preparation this is drawn from, the last two hours were four misses reviewed, two reference sections, and logistics. Then nothing.

## If you have more than five days

Do not stretch this plan. **Repeat days 1 to 3** with different material, then run day 4 once and day 5 once.

Spaced repetition beats a longer single pass, and re-testing misses two or three days later is what moves things into durable memory. Re-reading is comfort; re-testing is study.

---

## If you have less than five days

Compress in this order, dropping from the bottom:

1. **Never drop the blueprint diff.** Two hours that changes where every other hour goes.
2. **Never drop the multi-response drilling.** The cheapest marks on the paper.
3. **Never drop the framework items.** Pure recall, and unavailable to reasoning.
4. Drop the full-length paper before dropping any of the above — sit a 20-item proportional slice instead. You lose the pacing rehearsal, which is real, but pacing is a controllable risk and framework recall is not.

---

## The one thing not on this plan

Use Claude for real work while you do this, and pay attention while you use it.

Configure a Project properly and notice the difference. Catch a correction you keep repeating and move it into configuration — that instinct is a named exam objective. Notice Claude agreeing with a premise you fed it.

The exam tests applied judgment. Judgment comes from having made the calls, not from having read about them.
