Field Guide

Built backwards from a real objective-level breakdown. Most guides restate the blueprint. This one tells you which objectives actually get sampled, covers an item type that appears on roughly a sixth of the paper and almost nowhere in the literature, and names where confident preparation still leaves you exposed.

What makes this different

Everything here is calibrated against the objective-by-objective breakdown from one passing sitting, then grounded in the lesson material of four Anthropic courses rather than in help-centre articles. That breakdown showed something useful and slightly humbling: the objectives that scored lowest were the ones prepared hardest. Several sections below exist purely to close gaps that heavy, confident preparation still missed.

Weighting

Seven domains, twenty-seven objectives

Domain weights are published. What is not published is that a 60-item form spreads across roughly 27 objectives — about two items each. That has a direct consequence: a single objective you have never thought about costs you two items, and there is no way to make it up inside the same domain.

Under-documented and tested

The 4D Framework

This comes from Anthropic's own AI Fluency: Framework & Foundations course, and it does appear on the exam — typically as an item giving four options and asking which is most descriptive of one of the Ds. Almost no CCAO-F guide covers it, because the course lesson bodies sit behind enrolment. The structure below is from the public course page.

Delegation

Deciding · effectiveness

Deciding deliberately which parts of the work go to AI and which you keep for yourself. Happens before any prompt is written. Its three components are ordered — you understand the problem, then the tool, then divide the work.

  • Problem Awareness — grasping the goal, and what the work actually involves
  • Platform Awareness — knowing the capabilities of the systems available to you
  • Task Delegation — splitting the work between you and the system, deliberately

Description

Communicating · effectiveness

Communicating with AI clearly enough that it can act on what you actually meant. More than writing prompts — Academy stresses that these systems behave like collaborators you talk to, not lookup tables you query.

  • Product — what you want created
  • Process — how it should approach the request
  • Performance — how it should behave with you

Discernment

Evaluating · effectiveness

Assessing what AI produces, and how it went about producing it, with a critical eye. Academy presents it as the mirror image of Description — the three types map onto the three Descriptions exactly.

  • Product — quality of the output
  • Process — how it arrived there
  • Performance — how it behaved in the interaction

Diligence

Taking responsibility · ethics and safety

Engaging with AI responsibly. Academy draws a sharp line here: the other three competencies address effectiveness and efficiency, while Diligence addresses ethics and safety.

  • Creation — choosing which tools to use for a piece of work, and on what terms
  • Transparency — declaring where AI contributed and what it did
  • Deployment — owning, verifying and vouching for what you share

Two definitions worth getting exactly right

Creation Diligence is about tool choice, not craftsmanship. It covers which AI system you selected, what data you shared with it, and the privacy, security and ethical considerations in that choice. It is not "being careful while making the thing" — that reading is wrong and it is the natural misreading.

Verification lives in Deployment Diligence. Academy defines it as taking responsibility for the outputs you use or pass on — checking them, and then standing behind them. So checking a claim before circulating a document is Deployment Diligence — even though evaluating that same claim mid-draft would be Product Discernment. The discriminator is whether you are assessing or vouching.

The Description–Discernment loop · four named steps

Academy gives this loop an explicit four-step form, and it is the single most likely source of any ordering item built on the framework:

  1. Describe — state Product, Process and Performance
  2. Discern — evaluate the output, the approach, and the behaviour
  3. Refine — feed back, clarify or adjust the description, iterate
  4. Integrate — add your own expertise, decide what to keep, and take responsibility for the final output

Note what step 4 is doing. Integrate is where the loop hands off to Diligence: you decide what survives, what gets changed, what gets discarded, and you own the result. Most guides stop the loop at Refine, which misses the step that actually closes it.

The five-step sequence, now sourced

Put Delegation in front of the loop and you have a complete five-step task cycle, entirely from official material:

Delegate → Describe → Discern → Refine → Integrate

Academy also gives a five-step project planning sequence in the Delegation lesson: choose the project, establish the vision and what success looks like, identify the major tasks, analyse each task for human versus AI strengths, then create the plan. Both are five steps, both are official, and both run understand → specify → produce → evaluate → own.

How these items work

In an item asking which option best describes one of the Ds, the three wrong options are typically accurate descriptions of the other three. So there is nothing to reason out — you either know which D owns which verb or you guess. Memorise four verbs: Delegation decides. Description communicates. Discernment evaluates. Diligence takes responsibility.

Practice

Which best describes Delegation in the 4D framework?
  1. Communicating requirements to the AI clearly enough to act on.
  2. Deciding which parts of a task to assign to AI, informed by awareness of the problem and of the platform.
  3. Critically evaluating the AI's output before relying on it.
  4. Taking responsibility for how AI-assisted work is created, disclosed, and deployed.

B. A is Description, C is Discernment, D is Diligence. Note the shape: every distractor is a correct definition of a different D. Delegation's two awareness components are the tell — problem awareness and platform awareness both precede the work.

Performance Description refers to which of the following?
  1. Specifying the output you want the AI to produce.
  2. Specifying the method or steps the AI should follow.
  3. Specifying how the AI should behave, its role and manner.
  4. Specifying how the output will be measured after delivery.

C. The three Descriptions map cleanly: Product is what, Process is how it works, Performance is how it behaves. D is plausible-sounding but describes evaluation, which belongs to Discernment.

An associate reviews a draft, identifies that it buries the recommendation, and rewrites the prompt to lead with the decision. Which part of the framework does this illustrate?
  1. Task Delegation, since the associate is reassigning work.
  2. The Description–Discernment loop, since evaluation is feeding a revised description.
  3. Creation Diligence, since the associate is improving the artefact.
  4. Platform Awareness, since the associate is working within the tool's limits.

B. This is the loop by definition — discernment producing a better description. The trap is C, because "improving the work" sounds like diligence; Creation Diligence is about taking responsibility for how work is produced, not about the iteration mechanic itself.

Which is the clearest example of Transparency Diligence?
  1. Checking a cited statistic against its source before the report goes out.
  2. Telling a client, when asked, that AI assisted in preparing their deliverable.
  3. Choosing an approved enterprise tool over a personal account for client data.
  4. Deciding a task needs human expertise and keeping it in-house.

B. Transparency is specifically disclosure — being open about AI's role with those who need to know. Each distractor is a correctly-named different component: A is Deployment Diligence (verifying and vouching for what you share), C is Creation Diligence (which system you choose and what you share with it), D is Task Delegation. All four are good practice; only one is transparency.

Which of these is the named artefact Academy uses to practise Transparency Diligence?
  1. An audit log of every prompt used in producing the work.
  2. A diligence statement acknowledging AI's role and affirming your responsibility for the output.
  3. A model card describing the system's training and limitations.
  4. A disclosure notice naming the model version and tier used.

B. Academy's diligence statement combines the disclosure with the affirmation — it states which systems assisted and with what tasks, confirms the output was reviewed, and asserts that the content, its accuracy and the way it is presented remain your responsibility. Note it does both jobs at once: transparency about the role, and ownership of the result.

Which pairing correctly matches a D to its scope?
  1. Discernment covers only the accuracy of the final output.
  2. Diligence applies only once work is published externally.
  3. Discernment covers the output, the process, and the AI's behaviour.
  4. Delegation covers the wording of the request to the AI.

C. Discernment mirrors Description across all three dimensions — that symmetry is deliberate. A under-scopes it to Product Discernment alone, B under-scopes Diligence to Deployment alone, and D describes Description.

Where preparation fails

Three gaps confident study still leaves

On the sitting behind this guide, these three objectives were the weakest on the whole report — while closely related objectives in the same domains came back perfect. In each case the cause was the same: the study material taught one half of a two-part objective, and the candidate never noticed the other half existed.

Gap 1 · Organising information is not the same as choosing a format

This objective has two clauses: organising and curating the information that goes in, and choosing the format that comes out. Most preparation covers the second exhaustively and never touches the first. The curation half turns out to be well documented — it is just documented under Projects rather than under output formats, which is why it gets missed.

Curation, from Claude 101

Four practices the course sets out:

  • Start narrow, then widen. One project per line of work beats one project for everything.
  • Keep the knowledge base current. Stale documents produce stale answers. Review periodically.
  • Name documents descriptively — 2026-Q2-Supplier-Scorecard.xlsx, not data.xlsx — and keep related files together. Both the name of a file and the company it keeps are signals about how documents relate to one another.
  • Reference documents by name in the prompt to focus retrieval: "Using the Q3 pricing sheet, which segments moved most on discount?"

That third one is the non-obvious one and the most testable: filenames and file grouping are inputs to Claude's retrieval, not just housekeeping. Curation means shaping what Claude can find, not only what you include.

For the format half, there are four answers, not two — and Claude 101 draws the boundary explicitly. Office formats and PDFs are handled by a distinct file-creation capability, not by artifacts.

DeliverableFormat
Short answer or clarificationInline
Substantial, iterated, reusedArtifact
Records bound for another systemStructured data
Opened in Excel, Word, or a PDF readerGenerated file via code execution

An artifact containing a table is not a spreadsheet. If the recipient will pivot the data, the answer is a generated file.

Artifact triggers · Claude 101Artifact types · six named

Likely to be edited, iterated on, or reusedCode snippets

Publishing differs by plan and is worth knowing: Free, Pro and Max can publish publicly — reachable by anyone with the link, no Claude account required, not indexed by search engines, and it is only the version you pick that goes out, with the conversation itself staying private. Team and Enterprise share internally with authentication instead.

Gap 2 · Writing standing instructions is authoring, not sorting

Nearly all preparation on this objective teaches classification — what belongs in instructions versus knowledge sources, and at which scope. That is a different objective — the one about standing a Project up with its instructions and its knowledge sources — and that one is usually the one that comes back clean. The verb here is create. Expect to judge whether a given instruction is well written.

The four components · Claude 101

Claude 101 lists four things good project instructions typically include. This is the canonical answer set for the objective:

ComponentExample — written for this guide, not the course’s
Context"This project produces supplier evaluation summaries for the procurement team"
Process"Establish the evaluation criteria first, then score each supplier against them"
Tone and style"Plain, direct language. Assume the reader is not a procurement specialist"
Specific requirements"Close every summary with a recommendation and the single biggest risk"

Note the mapping: Process is Process Description, tone and style is Performance Description, specific requirements is Product Description. The instruction panel is the 4D Description competency at the "lasting" end of the spectrum.

Instructions can encode conditional workflows

This is the part that overturns the simple "concise durable rules" model. Claude 101 explicitly endorses using project instructions to automate a workflow, with a trigger and a response — the example it gives is a standing rule that turns any uploaded transcript into a summary on a fixed template. It frames instructions as programming the project’s behaviour. So a when-this-then-that rule is legitimate and intended. Concision still matters, but the reason to reject a long instruction is that it is vague or unfalsifiable, not that it contains a conditional.

  • Specific and checkable. Vague direction produces inconsistent output — "be professional" is not an instruction, "under 150 words, no jargon, lead with the recommendation" is.
  • Behaviour, not content. Reference documents belong in knowledge, not instructions.
  • Correctly scoped. Account-level for everything you do; Project-level for one workstream; the prompt for one conversation.
  • Durable. If it changes each cycle it is an input — though a conditional rule about how to handle recurring inputs is itself durable.
Which is the most effective Project instruction?
  1. "Always produce high-quality, professional output appropriate to the audience and context."
  2. "Write in British English. Executive summaries under 200 words, leading with the recommendation. No bulleted lists in client-facing text."
  3. "Refer to the Q3 pricing sheet, the brand guide, and last quarter's board pack when answering."
  4. "Handle pricing questions using the current rate card, unless the client is on legacy terms, in which case check with finance, and if the query concerns renewals then apply the renewal matrix instead."

B. Specific, durable, behavioural, checkable — it covers tone, format and a requirement. A is unfalsifiable, which is the real disqualifier. C points at knowledge sources rather than setting behaviour. D is the closest call: conditional rules are endorsed by Claude 101, so it is not wrong for being a conditional — it is wrong because three nested exceptions with an escalation path is a decision tree, not an instruction, and it will crowd out everything else in the panel.

Gap 3 · "Business and technical tasks"

The prompting objective covers business and technical tasks. Preparation material overwhelmingly uses business scenarios — campaigns, board reports, customer emails. The technical half gets skipped, and it is worth roughly one item. Practise prompts for specifying a data transformation, describing a calculation to be performed, defining a schema or output contract, and stating acceptance criteria for something that will be checked programmatically.

The general rule holds across both: name the audience, the decision the output must serve, the constraints, and the success criteria. Supply an exemplar when voice or format matters. Intensifiers — "be thorough", "try harder", "think outside the box" — add nothing.

The three-part prompt framework · Claude 101

Before the six techniques, there is a simpler official structure — and Claude 101 presents it as an adaptation of the 4D Framework. Three steps, in order:

  1. Setting the stage — who you are, what you are trying to achieve, and the background that bears on it
  2. Defining the task — the actual verb: draft this, analyse that, build the other
  3. Specifying rules — the constraints on style, register and shape, plus any examples worth attaching

Two things follow. It is an ordered three-step sequence, so it can appear as an ordering item. And Academy's guidance on register is worth remembering because it cuts against over-engineering: talk to Claude the way you would talk to a colleague — naturally and concisely, without ceremony.

The six official techniques

Academy names exactly six foundational prompting techniques. If this objective has a canonical answer set, this is it — and no other CCAO-F guide appears to list them.

TechniqueWhat it means
Give contextSay what you want, what it is for, and what background bears on it
Show examplesDemonstrate the style or shape you are after instead of describing it
Specify constraintsPin down length, format and anything else the output has to satisfy
Break into stepsWalk the model through the reasoning rather than asking for the end point
Ask it to think firstLeave room for the reasoning to happen before the answer arrives
Define role or toneSay who it is writing as, and in what register

Academy adds a seventh and calls it the secret weapon: have the model help you improve the prompt itself. Note the careful distinction from the self-assessment trap — asking Claude to improve a prompt is endorsed, while asking Claude to rate its own output is not. Those are different operations and an item can turn on the difference.

Which pair of techniques best addresses output that is accurate but arrives in a different structure each time?
  1. Give context and define role or tone.
  2. Show examples and specify constraints.
  3. Ask it to think first and break into steps.
  4. Define role or tone and ask it to think first.

B. Structural inconsistency is a Product Description problem, and the two techniques that pin down form are demonstrating it and stating it. C addresses reasoning quality, not output shape. A and D address manner and framing. Mapping a symptom to the right technique is the skill being tested — not reciting the six.

The pattern

Anthropic teaches in fours

Reading across Claude Academy, one structural habit stands out: the material is organised into named, enumerable frameworks — four Ds, four properties, four decision dimensions, three-part sub-components. This matters directly, because an item asking "which is most descriptive of X" is almost certainly drawing on one of them. Learn the named frameworks and you cover a category of item that pure judgement cannot.

Three ways of engaging with AI

Named in the same lesson as the 4Ds and easy to miss. The 4D competencies "apply across all three."

ModeDefinition
AutomationClaude carries out defined tasks from the instructions you give it
AugmentationYou and Claude work through the thinking and the doing together
AgencyYou set Claude up to work independently on your behalf, shaping what it knows and how it behaves rather than directing each action

The progression is one of decreasing turn-by-turn control. Note that Agency is defined as shaping knowledge and behaviour rather than actions — which is exactly what configuring a Project does. And AI Fluency itself is defined by four adjectives — working with AI effectively, efficiently, ethically and safely — four adjectives that map onto the four Ds in order.

One scoping point first. Academy is careful to point out that most AI in everyday use — spam filters, recommender systems, fraud detection — is not generative at all. Those classify and rank existing content; generative AI produces new content. Everything below applies to the generative kind only, which is a distinction worth having straight if an item contrasts the two.

Where Claude's character comes from · and the four fingerprints

Two training stages, and the second one leaves marks you can predict. Pretraining produces something closer to a very capable document completer: it predicts what comes next and has no notion of being helpful to you. Fine-tuning then layers assistant behaviour on top, using human judgments about what makes a good response — and that is where the marks come from.

FingerprintHow it shows up
SycophancyA pull toward validating your framing rather than challenging it
VerbosityA default toward length, even when one sentence would do
Over-cautionHedging that is reflexive rather than proportionate to real risk
Loose calibrationStated confidence sits loosely against actual reliability

This is why self-assessment never works

The guide has said all along that asking Claude to rate its own confidence is always the wrong answer. Here is the documented mechanism: stated confidence and actual reliability come apart, and that gap is a fingerprint of fine-tuning. Confidence is a trained-in conversational behaviour, not a measurement. So a high self-rating carries no information about accuracy — it was never wired to.

Sycophancy has the same root, which is why it is so hard to spot: the model was shaped by human preference judgments, and humans prefer being agreed with. That is also why it concentrates exactly where it hurts most — the course’s own reflection exercise asks where sycophancy would cost you most, and answers it: wherever you were hoping for a candid assessment.

The documented countermeasure

Sycophancy has a stated fix, and it is not "be more critical." It is an explicit invitation to disagree — a standing request, written into the prompt, to be told when you are wrong. Note this is the invite-pushback remedy named in the properties-collide lesson, and it belongs to Description, not Discernment: you are pre-authorising dissent in the prompt rather than catching flattery afterwards. If an item describes output that conveniently confirms the user's premise, the answer is likely to be either verifying independently or asking for the counter-case explicitly.

The 4 Properties of AI

A second four-part framework, parallel to the 4Ds and just as testable. Where the 4Ds describe how you should work, the properties describe how the model works — the mechanism behind every failure in the evaluation and troubleshooting domains. Summarised from the course:

PropertyWhat it means
Next token predictionProduces text one piece at a time on the basis of what usually follows what. Extraordinarily capable autocomplete, not retrieval
KnowledgeEverything it knows comes from training data with a fixed cut-off. What was in that data, and when the reading stopped
Working memoryWhatever the model is attending to has to fit a context window of fixed size. Leverage while you are inside it, a cliff when you are not
SteerabilityHonours an instruction by extending a pattern rather than by grasping a purpose. Very steerable, and always some distance between the intent and what lands

Academy teaches each property with the same five-part anatomy — a capability zone, a limitation zone, characteristic failures, the product features that exist to patch it, and a 4D connection. That last one is the part worth memorising, because it links this framework to the other one.

Each property also reduces to one question, which is the fastest way to hold all four: How is this answer being produced? (next token prediction) · What does it actually know? (knowledge) · What can it currently see? (working memory) · How much steering do I have? (steerability).

Calibrated trust

Each property is a continuum, and the same mechanism produces both the capability and the limitation. Which gives the course its definition of the skill: calibrated trust means placing a particular task somewhere along that continuum, rather than trusting or distrusting the system wholesale. That is the rigorous version of trust-is-a-dial — you are not deciding whether Claude is reliable, you are deciding where this task sits on four separate axes.

PropertyLimitation zoneCharacteristic failure4D link
Next token predictionNovel or sparse territory; anything needing "true" distinguished from "sounds true"FabricationDiscernment
KnowledgeAnything niche, local, contested, or later than the cut-offStaleness, uneven coverage, inherited defaultsDelegation
Working memoryLong documents and long conversations; assuming continuity between sessions; hiding the key detail in the middle of a large inputSilent truncationDescription
SteerabilityLong reasoning chains; abstract or ambiguous instructions; numerical or logical precisionReasoning drift, letter-over-spiritDescription

Four rules from this course that answer exam items directly

  • Fabrication clusters wherever a claim gets specific — a name, a date, a figure, a citation, a URL, a quotation. Precision is the signal to check, not a sign of reliability. That is a far sharper trigger than a general instruction to verify what matters.
  • Working memory has a cliff, not a gradient. The failure mode is silent truncation, and you will not always be warned. Nothing tells you the context was cut.
  • Correcting the model teaches it nothing. It responds only to what is in front of it in the current context. This is why correcting Claude repeatedly in a conversation is not a fix — the correction has to live in configuration.
  • When an instruction is followed to the letter and still misses the point, restate the goal rather than the instruction. Saying the same thing more forcefully does not close that gap. That is the letter-over-spirit failure, and it is the mechanism behind every "be more concise" item.

Note the fourth one carefully. It says restate the goal, not add more constraints — a subtler prescription than the guide's earlier "replace the vague directive with a hard constraint." Both work; naming the intent is the officially documented move.

When properties collide · the actual diagnostic skill

Academy is explicit that failures in practice usually involve two properties interacting rather than one, and it names two diagnostic pairs to recognise on sight:

PairPresents as
Next token prediction + KnowledgeHallucinated specifics — a confident, precise claim about something thinly represented in training
Working memory + SteerabilityLong-conversation drift — the constraint was given, and has quietly fallen out of attention

Once you can name which properties are in play, the remedy follows directly: verify the specifics, re-supply the context, hand the arithmetic to code execution, or invite pushback. Four properties, two pairs, four fixes — the course frames this as Discernment put to work. If an item describes a failure and asks for the cause or the remedy, this is the frame.

The Description spectrum

This is Anthropic's own framing of the distinction that dominates the configuration domain, and it is more useful than the durable-versus-per-instance rule because it has three levels rather than two. Academy sets out the range of ways you can shape what Claude gives you and orders them by durability:

LevelScopeExamples
BasicSingle turnIterating, adding context, uploading a file
MiddleModerate persistenceFeatures that hold for a stretch of work
LastingAll subsequent interactionsConfiguration — instructions, personalisation

Note that it sits under Description, not under some separate configuration heading. Choosing where a rule lives is a description decision — you are still communicating what you want, just at a longer time horizon.

Trust is a dial, not a switch

Academy's framing of the human-review question, and it is more precise than "when is review required." The instruction is to adjust your own level of scrutiny to the stakes, using a medical analogy: you would not question a doctor who suggested eating more carrots, but you would want a second opinion before surgery.

Practically: the same output warrants light review as an internal brainstorm and rigorous verification as a board input. The question is never "is Claude trustworthy" but "how consequential is this particular use."

A failure mode most guides omit

Academy names two ways answers go wrong: hallucination and sycophancy — the model agreeing with you, validating a premise, or softening a finding because that is what the conversation seems to want. It is harder to spot than fabrication precisely because it feels like agreement rather than error. If an item describes output that conveniently confirms what the user already believed, or a critique that has gone strangely gentle, sycophancy is the name for it.

Signature moves, and what does not transfer

Academy's research-backed curriculum gives each surface a signature move — the first habit worth building. In Chat it is iterating, refining through follow-up turns. In Cowork and Claude Code it is clarifying the goal before Claude executes, because you are handing off rather than steering turn by turn.

The most useful sentence on the whole site

Academy's finding is that discernment does not accumulate with time served, and it does not come along for the ride with product knowledge. Knowing the tool well does not make you better at judging what it produces. That has to be taught on purpose, which is the whole premise of this guide.

This predicts the exact shape of the breakdown behind this guide: every product and model-selection objective came back perfect, while evaluating accuracy and completeness, and deciding when review is required, both lagged well behind. Feature knowledge was strong and discernment trailed it, exactly as the research says it would. If you are preparing and feel confident because you know the features well, that is the warning sign rather than the reassurance.

The four building blocks of standing context

A fourth four-part framework, and the one that most directly reframes the configuration domain. Cowork's course names four ways Claude accumulates knowledge about your work, each carrying something different forward:

BlockWhat Claude learns
Global instructionsStanding facts about you and how you prefer to work
ProjectsEverything belonging to a single line of work
SkillsThe steps a particular process should follow
PluginsYour team's encoded expertise, packaged and distributed

The cleanest scope rule in any of the courses

Global instructions are about you. A project is about the work.

That one distinction answers every account-versus-project scope item, and it explains why rather than just stating the boundary. Global instructions carry who you are, the vocabulary you use, and the form you want answers in. A Project carries one stream of work.

The maintenance heuristic worth memorising

Cowork’s course is explicit that a set of global instructions is not meant to arrive finished. It grows: whatever you keep having to say again is the next thing that belongs in it. A correction you find yourself repeating is a configuration candidate. That is the documented answer to the objective on keeping configurations and instructions current — configuration is grown from observed friction, not designed in advance. It also confirms from the other direction why repeatedly correcting Claude in-conversation is not a fix.

A Cowork project has four parts — three you set up and one Claude builds: instructions scoped to the project, scheduled tasks that run with the project's context, context folders or links every conversation can reach, and memory, which accumulates as you work rather than being written by you. Note the contrast the course draws: in Chat, memory builds itself automatically, whereas in Cowork most of what carries from one task to the next is context you deliberately set up. Away from a project, every session begins from nothing except your global instructions.

Where the guide and the product have diverged

The published exam guide names the model tiers as "Haiku, Sonnet, Opus." Academy's current model tutorial is titled "Choosing the right Claude model: Haiku, Sonnet, Opus, or Fable." Learn tier positioning — fastest and cheapest, balanced, most capable — rather than a fixed list of names, because the list moves and the positioning does not.

The inference worth making

The two frameworks are one system

Read across the four properties and something falls out that neither course states outright: each property is the reason its matching D exists. The 4Ds are not an arbitrary list of good habits. They are the four competencies you need precisely because the model has those four properties.

Because the model…You need…Which is why
knows unevenly and is frozen at a cutoffDelegationyou must decide what to hand over and what to supply yourself
attends to a fixed window, and follows words not intentDescriptionhow you structure and phrase context is the whole lever
predicts plausible text rather than retrieving truthDiscernmentfluency is not accuracy, so output must be judged
does all of the above invisibly, at scale, in your nameDiligencethe responsibility cannot sit with the tool

Why this is worth holding

Two frameworks of four is eight things to memorise. One framework of four with a mechanism behind each is one thing to understand — and understanding survives paraphrase in a way memorised lists do not.

It also predicts the answer when an item gives you a symptom rather than a label. A confident, precise, unverifiable claim is next-token prediction meeting a thin patch of knowledge, so the response is Discernment — verify the specific. A constraint given forty messages ago and quietly dropped is working memory meeting steerability, so the response is Description — move the constraint into configuration rather than restating it. You are not matching keywords; you are reasoning from mechanism to competency to action.

And a fifth thing the properties do not cover

The four properties explain what the model can do. The four fine-tuning fingerprints — sycophancy, verbosity, over-caution, loose calibration — explain how it behaves while doing it. That distinction matters on the exam, because a fingerprint failure looks like a capability failure and is not one. Output that flatters your premise is not a knowledge gap. Output that runs long is not a steerability gap. Both are trained-in dispositions, and both are addressed in the prompt rather than by changing tier, tool, or model.

Flagged as inference: the property-to-D links are Academy's own, stated per lesson. Reading them as a single causal system, and setting the fingerprints alongside them as a separate axis, is this guide's synthesis rather than a claim either course makes.

The item type nobody prepares for

Sequencing items

You will get somewhere between five and ten items — potentially a sixth of the paper — that present five activities and ask which order performs the task correctly. The published guide describes only multiple-choice and multiple-response, so these arrive as multiple-choice, with five candidate orderings as the options rather than the usual four. Almost no preparation material covers them, which makes them the single highest-value thing on this page.

Why they are dangerous

They are not knowledge items. You can know every fact in this guide and still lose eight items here, because the skill being tested is whether you understand that business and AI workflows have a dependency order — and that most wrong orderings are wrong for one specific, findable reason.

The spine

Nearly every sequence on this exam is a specialisation of one shape. Learn the shape and you can reconstruct any particular sequence.

  1. Understand — what is this for, what decision does it serve, who is it for
  2. Frame — scope, structure, criteria for success
  3. Gather — the inputs the work draws on
  4. Produce — the draft, the design, the analysis
  5. Check — against sources, against scope, against criteria
  6. Adapt — for the audience who will actually read it
  7. Approve — the named human who owns the release
  8. Deploy and measure — pilot, then scale, then check against the baseline

Ten ordering rules

  • Purpose is always first. Nothing precedes knowing what the work is for. If one activity defines the objective, it goes first — no exceptions.
  • Who before what. Audience or persona precedes content, because it determines what the content should be.
  • Frame before fill. Structure, outline, or stages come before gathering detail to put in them.
  • Gather before generate. You cannot analyse evidence you have not collected.
  • Enumerate before evaluate. List the risks before scoring them, map the steps before classifying them.
  • Fix accuracy before polishing tone. Correctness precedes audience adaptation.
  • Adapt before approve. The approver must see the version that ships.
  • Approve before distribute. Always.
  • Pilot before scale. And capture the baseline before you change anything, or the pilot has nothing to measure against.
  • Verify the fix before institutionalising it. Confirm it worked, then set the review cycle that prevents recurrence.

The three traps

Wrong orderings on this exam are rarely random. They are usually one of these:

TrapWhat it looks like
Premature approvalSign-off placed before the last edit, so the owner approved a document that then changed
Acting before diagnosingChanging a variable before forming a hypothesis about what is wrong
Prevention before verificationSetting up the review cycle before confirming the fix actually worked

The engagement mode sets the shape

This is the key that makes ordering items tractable. Academy states the 4D competencies "apply across all three" engagement modes — but the shape of the work differs by mode, and so does the sequence. Read the scenario, identify the mode, and you know which sequence family you are in before you look at the options.

ModeSequence shapeTell in the stem
AutomationDefine the task → specify instructions and constraints → execute → verify → useA one-off deliverable produced to instruction
AugmentationDescribe → Discern → Refine → Integrate, iteratingWorking alongside Claude, several turns, thinking partner
AgencyDefine purpose → establish knowledge → establish behaviour → operate → maintainSetting something up that will run repeatedly without you

Note what the Agency row actually is. Academy’s definition turns on two verbs: you configure the system to act on your behalf, establishing what it knows and how it behaves instead of handing it individual tasks. That is the configuration domain in one sentence. If a stem describes standing something up that others will use later, that is the family.

The official Project setup order · and it is not what you would guess

Claude 101 gives three numbered steps: Step 1 set up the project (name, description, visibility) → Step 2 add project instructions → Step 3 build your knowledge base.

Instructions come before knowledge. Most people — this guide included, until now — would order it the other way, on the reasoning that you gather the material and then write rules about it. The official sequence is behaviour first, content second. If a Project-setup ordering item appears, this is the answer, and the intuitive order is the trap.

Three questions, in order

Which engagement mode is this? Then, does the activity match a named framework? Then, and only then, fall back on the generic slots.

Most candidates skip straight to the third question, which is why these items eat clock. The first question takes five seconds and usually eliminates half the answer space.

Where the sequences actually come from

This is the part most guides get wrong, including an earlier draft of this one. Sequencing items are not testing Claude-specific knowledge. They are testing whether you know how the underlying business activity is properly conducted — and the Claude layer only determines where the human checkpoints sit. A journey map is sequenced the way journey maps have always been sequenced, whether or not AI is involved.

So the material to know is standard professional practice. These four are the ones worth holding, and only the last is Anthropic's own.

FrameworkSequenceShows up in

Notice what they share. Every one of them runs understand → specify → produce → check → embed. DMAIC makes the last step explicit and names it Control — putting something in place so the improvement holds. That is the same move as setting a review cycle after fixing a stale Project, and it is the step candidates most often place too early.

Anthropic's own interaction pattern

Every use-case walkthrough on Claude Academy is structured identically, and the first four steps are a genuine workflow worth knowing:

Describe the task — state what you are evaluating and the criteria that matter. Give Claude context — upload the files, enable the connectors. What Claude creates — the output. Follow up — refine, extend, or convert into something reusable.

Note that criteria come before context, and context before output. That ordering is Anthropic's, not an inference, and it maps onto Description then Discernment in the 4D framework.

A working shortcut, honestly labelled

Inference, not doctrine

What follows is a pattern observed across the eight practice items below, which were written for this guide rather than drawn from any published source. Two official five-step sequences now exist in the framework section above — Delegate, Describe, Discern, Refine, Integrate and the project-planning sequence — and where an item matches either of those, use the official one. The slots below are the fallback for activities that match no named framework.

The pattern: slot 1 is an understand-or-define activity, and slots 4 and 5 are usually a prove-then-commit pair. Which leaves slots 2 and 3 as the real decision — a two-way choice rather than a hundred-and-twenty-way one.

SlotWhat tends to live there
1 · UnderstandDefine the purpose, map the current state, or notice the problem
2 · Who or whichPersona, audience, segment, classification, owner
3 · Shape or sourceStructure, stages, design, instructions, evidence gathered
4 · ProveVerify, test, pilot, confirm, compare against criteria
5 · CommitApprove, release, scale, institutionalise

Tie-breaker for slots 2 and 3: who before shape, shape before content. The audience determines the structure, and the structure determines what fills it. In a journey map that is persona, then stages, then evidence per stage — never evidence first, because you would not know what to gather it against.

The one exception

Iterative sequences end on revise, not on commit, because the Description–Discernment loop is cyclical rather than terminal. If the five activities describe improving a prompt or a draft rather than shipping something, expect compare-then-revise as the closing pair.

Working an item under time pressure

Ask first whether the activity matches a framework above. If it does, use the framework — that is knowledge, not guesswork. If it does not, fall back on the slots: find the opener and strike every option that does not begin with it, assume the closing pair and strike again, then settle slots 2 and 3 with who-before-shape.

Either route is three small judgements rather than one large one. That matters because these items eat clock, and clock is the resource you are shortest of.

Hard rule: if a sequencing item passes ninety seconds, commit to your best ordering, flag it, and move on. At five to ten items they can quietly consume a third of the paper. The candidate whose report this guide is built on passed comfortably and still reports these as where most of the time went — they are the clock risk on this exam, not the knowledge risk.

Worth noticing

The 4D framework is itself a sequence: Delegation, then Description, then Discernment, then Diligence. Decide what to hand over, communicate it, evaluate what comes back, take responsibility for the result. If a sequencing item is built on the framework, that is the order.

The core

Four rules that settle most items

  1. Structure beats cleverness. Torn between a better prompt and a verification, governance, or configuration step? Take the mechanism.
  2. Accurate is not usable. Output can be wholly correct and still unfit — exceeding the sender's authority, burying a material conflict, omitting something silently, or landing at the wrong level for its reader.
  3. The model is rarely the answer. Right about one time in ten, and only on evidence of a capability ceiling: failure specifically on harder cases, after refinement has genuinely been tried.
  4. Escalate when it is not yours; diagnose when it is. Escalate for API work, fine-tuning, production architecture, legal or regulatory liability. Not merely because something is unfamiliar.

The single most missed distinction

When policy assigns work to another function, the question is who owns this document — not whether you can make the draft accurate. "Draft it, then have legal review it" feels diligent and is wrong: the framing is already decided by the time they see it. Verification is the right instinct almost everywhere else, which is exactly why it misfires here.

Research and extended thinking combine — Claude 101 notes that the two work together, so Claude can plan a line of enquiry before it begins searching.

Tier guidance, as Academy actually states it

Sonnet is the recommended default and the right choice for most everyday work. Opus is positioned for the most complex tasks. Haiku serves high-volume formulaic work. Extended thinking adds latency and is not needed for simple questions — so the documented escalation path is start on Sonnet with thinking off, then raise the model or enable thinking if you need more support.

This is a real nuance against the tidy "match the tier to the task" rule. There is an official default, and it is Sonnet. For a mixed workload, routing still applies — cheap tier for the routine majority, capable tier for the flagged minority — but "always use the best" and "default to the middle without thinking" remain wrong for different reasons: the first wastes budget, the second skips the judgement.

Configuration

What goes where

AskThen
What scope?Everywhere in your work → account level. Every conversation in one workstream → Project instructions. Just this once → the prompt.
Durable or per-instance?Templates, standing definitions, tone and format rules → configuration. This month's figures, today's question, this cycle's recipient → supplied each time.

Behavioural rules go in instructions; source documents go in knowledge. Project configuration belongs to the Project, not the user, so a new joiner needs no personal setup. Drift in a shared Project is fixed by ownership plus a change process, not by locking editing down.

Three levels, not two — Owner is a distinct level, and an earlier version of this guide listed only the first two. Projects also scale automatically: as the knowledge base approaches the context limit, Claude stops loading everything and starts searching the files for what is relevant instead — the course puts the resulting gain in capacity at up to an order of magnitude. You get a visual indicator; the experience is otherwise unchanged.

Governance

What the control attaches to

The subtlest family on the exam. Redaction is sometimes the answer and sometimes the trap, and which one depends entirely on what the rule attaches to.

TriggerResponse
Personal or regulated identifiersDe-identify before providing
A prohibited document classRedaction does not cure it. A redacted customer contract is still a customer contract
Contractual restriction, no personal dataCheck the agreement and get approval. Sensitivity is not only about personal data
Use beyond the collection purposePurpose limitation applies; needs review
Legal or regulatory liabilityEscalate to the owners. Do not draft first

Policy is not outcome-dependent — a skipped review is a violation even when nothing went wrong. Scope calibrates the depth of review, never whether it applies. Work material on a personal account sits outside the organisation's controls whoever pays for it. Improperly obtained input stays improper. Judgement stays human: Claude words an assessment, the manager forms it.

When the tool is the wrong tool · from the Cowork course

A rare thing in vendor material — an explicit list of when not to use the product. Three cases:

  • Regulated workflows needing an audit trail. Cowork activity does not appear in audit logs or data exports.
  • Anything you would not hand to a capable colleague and then walk away from. Signing off the contract, publishing the announcement, releasing the change customers will see. Claude prepares; you are the one who ships.
  • Highly sensitive personal data, anywhere beyond the perimeter your IT function has signed off on.

That middle test is the most portable heuristic in any of the four courses, and it generalises well beyond Cowork. If you would not hand it to a capable new colleague and walk away, it needs a human checkpoint — and the dividing line is preparation versus shipping.

Why controls actually fail

Most mistakes are not safeguards failing. They are someone clicking through a confirmation that did not quite say what they thought it said.

The practical moves follow from that. Point Claude at a dedicated working folder rather than a catch-all, because the folder is the boundary. Back up anything irreplaceable outside it first. Test new workflows on copies. Name destructive verbs precisely, since "cut the section" and "update the file" each carry an irrecoverable reading. State the bounds in the prompt. Have scheduled tasks draft for review before they act on your behalf. Then three checks while it runs: read the plan Claude sets out, watch for the scope widening, and actually read confirmations instead of clicking past them. The course is worth quoting on the last of these in spirit if not in letter — a vague sense that something is wrong is data, not noise.

Evaluation

Naming the failure

TypeTell
HallucinationSomething asserted is false. A plausible specific that does not exist
BiasA systematic skew the source did not support. Nothing false — the weighting was imported
IncompletenessSomething is missing and nothing signals it. The dangerous one
InconsistencyDifferent answers to the same question. Expected, not a fault to report
SycophancyAgrees with you, validates your premise, or softens a finding because the conversation invited it. Feels like agreement, not error
Silent truncationContext exceeded the window and was cut with no warning. A cliff, not a gradient — the output looks complete
Letter-over-spiritThe instruction was honoured and the intent was not. Restate the goal; repeating the instruction harder will not close the gap

One fabricated citation implicates the document — check the others. Never accept self-assessment: asking how confident it is, asking it to check its own accuracy, or regenerating to see whether the figure recurs are all non-verification. Human review is required for external publication under the organisation's name, legal or financial or regulatory consequence, material effect on an individual, or a reader who cannot judge accuracy themselves. A better model never substitutes for a review control.

Troubleshooting

Diagnostic order

Work down. The answer is almost always above the tier.

  1. Is a requirement vague, buried, or unmeasurable? Make it explicit and structured.
  2. Do two instruction layers conflict — prompt against Project? Reconcile them rather than re-prompting around it.
  3. Has the configuration changed — edited instructions, a superseded source, a connector failing quietly?
  4. Is a long conversation crowding out the constraints? Carry forward what still stands and start fresh.
  5. Does it fail only on harder cases, after real refinement? Now consider the tier.
  6. Is it outside your scope? Escalate.

Optimise by removing work, not speeding it up. If a step is duplicated, eliminate it — put the template in the configuration so output arrives already conforming. And when carrying context forward, take only the conclusions that still stand: summarising the whole conversation preserves the abandoned half too.

In the room

Mechanics

  • 2:00 per item. At sixty minutes you should be at or past item 30.
  • Read the stated count on every multi-response item, then count your letters before moving on. Partial selections score zero — the same as wrong. This is the most common way well-prepared candidates lose marks.
  • Never leave a blank. There is no penalty for a wrong answer.
  • Read the qualifier — FIRST, most appropriate, most important, independently. Several options can be true at once.
  • Two options both look right? Take the one that adds a control or fixes a structure over the one that improves a prompt or changes the model.
  • An option says escalate? Outside your scope or authority, probably right. Merely unfamiliar, probably not.
  • Over 2:30 on an item? Best guess, flag it, move on.

Honesty

What this guide cannot tell you
