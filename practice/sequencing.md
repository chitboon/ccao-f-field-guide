# Practice · Sequencing Items

**18 items.** Five activities, **five candidate orderings**, one correct sequence.

**Why this set exists.** This item type can account for a meaningful share of the paper and is almost absent from other prep material. It is also the clock risk: candidates consistently report these consuming disproportionate time, partly because **the stems are long**. Reading is a real part of the difficulty, and the items here are written at full length for that reason.

**Note on the option count.** Five orderings, not four. That matters more than it sounds: with five options a single confident elimination leaves you at one in four rather than one in three, so the eliminations are worth more and guessing is worth less. It also means at least two distractors usually differ from the answer by a single adjacent swap, which is where the marks are lost.

**The method, in three questions:**

1. **Which engagement mode is this?** Automation, Augmentation or Agency. Five seconds, and it usually narrows the answer space sharply.
2. **Does the activity match a named framework?** The Description–Discernment loop, project planning, DMAIC, journey mapping, design thinking. If so, use it — that is knowledge, not guesswork.
3. **Only then, fall back on slots.** Position 1 is almost always an understand-or-define activity; positions 4 and 5 are usually a prove-then-commit pair; the real decision is 2 and 3, settled by *who before shape, shape before content.*

**Check the last two positions first.** With five options, the fastest single cut is to look at what each ordering ends on. Any option that commits, releases, publishes, hands over or institutionalises before the step that proves it is wrong, and that usually removes two of the five before you have thought about the opening at all.

**Hard rule:** past ninety seconds on one of these, commit to your best ordering, flag it, and move on.

**Recurring traps:** `[approval]` sign-off before the last edit · `[acting]` changing before diagnosing · `[prevention]` institutionalising a fix before verifying it · and across every tag, committing or releasing before the step that proves it

**1.** `[journey]` You are an associate in the customer operations team of a mid-sized software company. The Head of Support has asked you to produce a customer journey map for the onboarding experience. The leadership team will use it to decide where to direct investment next quarter, and you intend to work through it with Claude. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Validate the completed map with the stakeholders who own each touchpoint, confirming it reflects how the process actually runs
2. Identify the customer personas whose onboarding journey is being mapped
3. Establish what decision the leadership team needs the map to inform
4. Gather evidence for each stage from support tickets and product analytics
5. Map the stages and touchpoints that each persona passes through

A. 3, 2, 4, 5, 1 · B. 2, 3, 5, 1, 4 · C. 3, 2, 5, 4, 1 · D. 3, 5, 2, 4, 1 · E. 2, 5, 4, 3, 1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C — 3, 2, 5, 4, 1.**

An Augmentation-mode task, and the order is a chain of dependencies rather than a convention.

Step 3 comes first because **Delegation begins with Problem Awareness** — grasping the goal, and what the work actually involves, before Claude is involved at all. Until you know which investment decision the map serves, you cannot judge which personas are in scope, and you cannot tell Claude what a good map looks like.

Personas precede stages because the stages *differ by persona*, and stages precede evidence because you need the frame before you know what to gather against. Validation is last: it is the point at which the artefact stops being a draft, which makes it the **Deployment Diligence** step.

**A is the evidence-before-frame trap.** Collecting tickets and analytics before the stages exist gives you a pile of material with nowhere to put it, and it quietly lets the available data decide the shape of the map.

Of the others, B opens with personas rather than purpose, and then validates the map with stakeholders before the evidence behind it has been gathered; D maps stages before establishing whose journey is being mapped; E establishes the decision fourth, after the map has already been built to answer an unstated question.
</details>

**2.** `[agency]` You are an associate who produces the same monthly operations report for a regional team. Every month you re-paste the same metric definitions and reporting template into a fresh conversation. You decide to set up a Claude Project so the context persists. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Upload the metric definitions, template and reference documents as knowledge
2. Assign an owner for the Project and agree how often its contents are reviewed
3. Create the Project, giving it a name, a description and a visibility setting
4. Write the Project instructions covering role, tone, format and standing constraints
5. Run a representative month's report through it and check the output

A. 3, 4, 1, 2, 5 · B. 1, 3, 4, 5, 2 · C. 3, 1, 4, 5, 2 · D. 3, 4, 5, 1, 2 · E. 3, 4, 1, 5, 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E — 3, 4, 1, 5, 2.**

This is **Agency mode** by definition: you are configuring Claude to work independently on your behalf, setting up its knowledge and its behaviour rather than handing it individual tasks.

Be clear about what kind of item this is. Only the first and last positions follow from dependencies; the middle is **documented convention**, and you either know it or you guess. The documented setup order is create the Project, **add instructions, then build the knowledge base.** Most people assume you gather the material first and then write rules about it, which is why the wrong order feels right. Behaviour is configured first, content second — memorise it rather than reasoning about it.

Testing then confirms it works, and ownership plus a review cycle comes last because that is the **maintenance** provision — the objective on keeping configurations and instructions current, and DMAIC's *Control* step under another name.

**A is the governance-before-verification trap.** Naming an owner for something unproven hands a colleague a maintenance duty over a configuration that may not function. Verification comes before you institutionalise anything.

Of the others, B creates knowledge before the Project exists to hold it; C loads the knowledge first and writes the instructions about it afterwards — the intuitive order, and the one the documented sequence reverses; D tests before there is anything for the test to draw on.
</details>

**3.** `[approval]` You are an associate in a compliance function. A revised expenses policy has been issued and you have used Claude to draft a plain-language summary for circulation to roughly four hundred staff. The policy owner in Finance must sign off before anything goes out. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Circulate the summary to all staff
2. Establish what the summary must cover and which audience it is written for
3. Verify each claim and the coverage of the summary against the source policy
4. Adjust the reading level and tone for a general staff audience
5. Obtain the Finance policy owner's approval for release

A. 2, 3, 4, 5, 1 · B. 2, 4, 3, 5, 1 · C. 2, 3, 5, 4, 1 · D. 3, 2, 4, 5, 1 · E. 2, 3, 4, 1, 5

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A — 2, 3, 4, 5, 1.**

Criteria, verify, adapt, approve, circulate. The first three map cleanly onto the framework: step 2 is **Product Description** (what the deliverable must be and for whom), step 3 is **Product Discernment** (checking the output against its source), step 4 is the audience-adaptation objective — same facts, different framing.

Steps 5 and 1 are **Deployment Diligence**: taking responsibility for what you put your name to and vouching for it before it circulates.

Note why verification sits *before* the reading-level edit rather than after it. Step 4 changes register, not claims, so a verified summary stays verified through it. Approval is different in kind — it is accountability for the artefact as released — which is why nothing at all may move after step 5.

**C is the premature-approval trap.** Approving before the final edit means what circulates is not what was approved. Approval must be the last thing before release, without exception.

Of the others, B simplifies for a general audience before checking the substance, so a claim that is wrong gets rewritten into something plainer and harder to spot; D begins verifying before establishing what the summary was meant to cover; E circulates to four hundred staff and collects the policy owner's approval afterwards.
</details>

**4.** `[acting]` You are an associate who runs a weekly summarisation task in a Claude Project. The Project instructions require four named sections in every summary, but for the past fortnight the summaries have been arriving with one section missing. The prompt has not changed. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Change one element of the prompt and re-run the same case
2. Reproduce the failure on a single short document you can run repeatedly
3. Notice that the required section is consistently absent from the output
4. Check whether the requirement is buried among the other instructions
5. Confirm the fix holds across the wider set of weekly documents

A. 3, 2, 1, 4, 5 · B. 3, 2, 4, 1, 5 · C. 3, 2, 4, 5, 1 · D. 3, 4, 2, 1, 5 · E. 2, 3, 4, 1, 5

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B — 3, 2, 4, 1, 5.**

Notice, reproduce, hypothesise, change one variable, confirm at scale — the documented troubleshooting order.

The framework tells you what to suspect. A requirement given and then silently dropped is a **Steerability** failure, and specifically the pattern where an instruction is followed to the letter but not in spirit, or lost among competing instructions. Step 4 tests that most likely cause first.

Note that reproduction precedes hypothesis. You cannot reason reliably about a failure you cannot yet trigger on demand, and the change-one-variable discipline in step 1 only means anything once you have a stable case to change it against.

**A is the acting-before-diagnosing trap.** Changing the prompt is the satisfying move and the premature one. If it happens to work you have learned nothing about why, and if it does not you have added a variable to a failure you had not yet characterised.

Of the others, C confirms across the wider document set before making any change to confirm; D forms a hypothesis before it can reproduce the failure the hypothesis is about; E begins reproducing a failure it has not yet noticed.
</details>

**5.** `[prevention]` You are an associate maintaining a shared Claude Project used by six colleagues for customer pricing queries. Colleagues have begun reporting that Claude's answers quote a rate card that was superseded last quarter. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Replace the outdated pricing documents with the current versions
2. Notice that answers are citing figures that are no longer current
3. Identify which of the Project's knowledge sources are out of date
4. Confirm that the corrected Project now answers with current figures
5. Set a review cycle so the knowledge base does not drift again

A. 2, 3, 5, 1, 4 · B. 2, 3, 1, 5, 4 · C. 3, 2, 1, 5, 4 · D. 2, 3, 1, 4, 5 · E. 2, 1, 3, 4, 5

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D — 2, 3, 1, 4, 5.**

Notice, diagnose, fix, verify, prevent recurrence — and each step maps to something named.

Steps 2 to 4 are the **maintenance** half of configuration and knowledge management: a knowledge base has to be kept current, because stale documents produce stale answers. Step 1 fixes the source of truth rather than patching each conversation, which matters because **correcting the model teaches it nothing** — it responds only to what is in front of it.

Step 5 is DMAIC's **Control**, the step candidates most often place too early. It is also the documented instinct that a correction you keep giving is a configuration candidate.

**B is the prevention-before-verification trap, and the one to study.** A review cycle built around an unverified fix locks in whatever is wrong with it. Verify, then institutionalise — never the reverse. Two other orderings make the same mistake more obviously; this one is a single adjacent swap from the answer, which is the version that actually costs marks.

Of the others, A sets the review cycle before the outdated documents have been replaced at all; C audits the knowledge sources before anyone has noticed a problem with them; E replaces documents before identifying which ones are stale.
</details>

**6.** `[augment]` You are an associate asked to produce a competitor positioning analysis for a product review meeting. You have the source material and intend to work through it with Claude as a thinking partner rather than handing it over wholesale. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Add your own market knowledge and decide what to keep, change or discard
2. Evaluate the output, the approach it took, and how it engaged with you
3. State the output you want, how it should approach it, and how it should behave
4. Adjust your description based on what the evaluation surfaced
5. Decide which parts of the analysis to work on with Claude and which to keep yourself

A. 5, 3, 2, 4, 1 · B. 5, 3, 2, 1, 4 · C. 5, 3, 4, 2, 1 · D. 3, 5, 2, 4, 1 · E. 3, 2, 4, 1, 5

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A — 5, 3, 2, 4, 1.**

This is the **four-step Description–Discernment loop** with the Delegation decision standing in front of it: Delegate, then Describe → Discern → Refine → Integrate. Keep the two apart — Delegation is a separate competency, not a step inside the loop, which is why the loop has four steps while this item has five activities. Step 5 is the Delegation decision, step 3 is Description in all three of its parts (product, process, performance), step 2 is Discernment in all three of *its* parts, step 4 is Refine, and step 1 is **Integrate** — bringing your own judgement to bear, deciding what survives, and owning the result.

Integrate is the step most accounts of the loop leave out, and it is where the loop hands off to Diligence.

**B is the integrate-too-early trap.** Integrate is where you take responsibility for the final output, so nothing can follow it. An option that puts a refinement step after it has you owning a result you then keep changing.

Of the others, C inverts Refine and Discern, refining on the basis of an evaluation not yet made; D begins describing before deciding what is being delegated at all; E places the delegation decision last, after the work it was supposed to scope is finished.
</details>

**7.** `[automation]` You are an associate who has been asked to produce a one-page briefing note summarising three vendor proposals for a manager who will read it before a meeting tomorrow. The task is well defined and you intend to give Claude clear instructions rather than work through it iteratively. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Send the briefing note to the manager
2. Check the note against the criteria you set before you asked for it
3. Specify the format, the length and what a complete answer must contain
4. Establish what the note is for and what decision the manager is making
5. Have Claude produce the draft from the three proposals

A. 4, 5, 3, 2, 1 · B. 3, 4, 5, 2, 1 · C. 4, 3, 5, 1, 2 · D. 4, 3, 2, 5, 1 · E. 4, 3, 5, 2, 1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E — 4, 3, 5, 2, 1.**

Define, specify, generate, check, use — the **Automation-mode** shape, where Claude completes a defined task from your instructions rather than collaborating turn by turn.

Steps 4 and 3 are Description, split the way Claude 101 splits it: setting the stage (the purpose and the decision it serves), then specifying the rules (format, length, completeness). Step 2 is **Product Discernment**, and it depends on step 3 having happened — you cannot check output against criteria you never stated, which is why the criteria are set before generation rather than after.

**C is the check-after-send trap.** Discernment that happens after delivery is not a control, it is an apology. The check has to sit between generation and use.

Of the others, A specifies the rules after the draft already exists, which makes them a review checklist rather than a brief; B inverts the opening pair, setting constraints before the purpose they serve is known; D attempts to check an output that has not yet been produced.
</details>

**8.** `[dmaic]` You are an associate in a finance operations team. The monthly close currently takes six working days and produces recurring errors that require rework. You have been asked to improve it, using Claude where it helps. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Put a control in place so the improvement holds after you move on
2. Record the current cycle time and error rate as a baseline
3. Define the problem and what a successful close would look like
4. Implement the change to the process
5. Analyse where the time and the errors actually accumulate

A. 3, 2, 4, 5, 1 · B. 3, 2, 5, 1, 4 · C. 3, 2, 5, 4, 1 · D. 3, 5, 2, 4, 1 · E. 2, 3, 5, 4, 1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C — 3, 2, 5, 4, 1.**

This is **DMAIC**: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control. It is not a Claude framework — it is standard process-improvement practice, and that is the point. Sequencing items test whether you know how the underlying business activity is properly conducted; the Claude layer only decides where the human checkpoints sit.

Two dependencies do the work. The baseline in step 2 must be captured **before** any change, or the improvement has nothing to be measured against. And **Control comes last** — the step candidates most often place too early, because it feels like setup when it is actually what makes the gain durable.

**B is the Control-before-Improve trap.** A control on an unchanged process locks in the six-day close. Control is the fifth step in DMAIC for a reason, and this is the single most common way candidates get the acronym wrong under time pressure.

Of the others, A implements before the analysis that would tell it what to change; D analyses before there is any measurement to analyse; E measures before defining what is being measured for.
</details>

**9.** `[agency]` You are an associate who wants a status summary pulled together from a shared folder every Friday afternoon without you having to ask. You are setting this up as a scheduled task that will run while you are not watching. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Point the task at the live folder
2. Decide what the summary should contain and which sources it draws from
3. Run it once against a copy of the folder rather than the real one
4. Check what the first run actually produced
5. Constrain it to producing a draft you review rather than acting in your name

A. 2, 3, 5, 4, 1 · B. 2, 5, 3, 4, 1 · C. 5, 2, 3, 4, 1 · D. 2, 5, 1, 3, 4 · E. 2, 3, 4, 5, 1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B — 2, 5, 3, 4, 1.**

Define the task, constrain it to drafting, test on a copy, check the result, then point it at live data. Steps 5, 3 and 1 are all **Deployment Diligence** — taking responsibility for something that will act in your name while you are not present.

The documented practice is explicit on both points. Until an unattended task has proved it behaves the way you need, keep it to drafting rather than letting it act in your name; and let its first run work on a copy, not on the real data.

**D is the live-data-first trap.** The copy exists so that a misconfigured first run is recoverable. An ordering that reaches the live folder before the rehearsal has thrown away the only cheap chance to find out what the task actually does.

Of the others, A also tests before the draft-only constraint is in place, with the safeguard added after the risk has already been taken; C constrains a task that has not been defined yet; E tests before constraining the task to drafting, so the very first unattended run could act rather than draft.
</details>

**10.** `[design]` You are an associate asked to help design a new internal request process for a service team that currently handles requests inconsistently by email. The team lead will own whatever process is adopted. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Test a draft process with the people who will actually run it
2. Understand how requests are handled today and where they go wrong
3. Draft two or three concrete process designs for the team lead to weigh
4. Define what the new process must achieve and for whom
5. Generate a wide range of options without filtering for feasibility yet

A. 2, 4, 3, 5, 1 · B. 4, 2, 5, 3, 1 · C. 2, 5, 4, 3, 1 · D. 2, 4, 5, 3, 1 · E. 2, 4, 5, 1, 3

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D — 2, 4, 5, 3, 1.**

Design thinking: **Empathise, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test.** Understand the current state, define the requirement, generate options divergently, narrow to concrete drafts, then test with the people who will run it.

Note step 5 and its deliberate ordering against step 3. Ideation comes before prototyping because filtering too early collapses the option space — the same distinction drawn between brainstorming prompts, which defer feasibility, and analysis prompts, which apply criteria.

Note also what the Associate is doing throughout: surfacing options and drafting alternatives. **The team lead decides.** Claude and the Associate support; the accountable person owns the design.

**E is the nothing-to-test trap.** Testing a set of unfiltered options is a workshop, not a prototype test. The prototype step exists to give the team something specific enough to react to.

Of the others, A drafts concrete designs before generating options, collapsing ideation into prototyping; B defines requirements before understanding the current state they are meant to fix; C generates options before knowing what they must achieve.
</details>

**11.** `[approval]` You are an associate at a consultancy. You have used Claude extensively to draft a client deliverable, and the engagement partner must approve it before it goes to the client. The firm discloses AI assistance in client work. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Send the deliverable to the client
2. Verify the factual claims against the underlying source material
3. Draft the deliverable with Claude's assistance
4. Add the statement disclosing AI's role and your responsibility for the content
5. Have the engagement partner review and approve it

A. 3, 2, 4, 5, 1 · B. 2, 3, 4, 5, 1 · C. 3, 2, 5, 4, 1 · D. 3, 4, 2, 5, 1 · E. 3, 2, 4, 1, 5

**A — 3, 2, 4, 5, 1.**

Draft, verify, disclose, approve, send. Step 2 is **Product Discernment**, step 4 is **Transparency Diligence** — being open about the part AI played in the work — and steps 5 and 1 are **Deployment Diligence**.

**C is the premature-approval trap in a subtler dress.** What gets appended after sign-off here is precisely the accountability language the partner was being asked to stand behind. That is the worst possible thing to add post-approval.

Of the others, B attempts to verify claims in a deliverable that does not exist yet; D attaches a statement asserting that you have reviewed the content before you have verified any of it, which makes the disclosure itself untrue at the moment it is written; E sends to the client and obtains the partner's approval afterwards.

**12.** `[governance]` You are an associate who has received a dataset from a commercial partner for a market analysis. The data contains no personal information, but it arrived under a data-sharing agreement your organisation signed. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Run the analysis
2. Establish what use the data-sharing agreement actually permits
3. Recognise that this dataset is governed by a partner agreement
4. Obtain written approval for the intended use from whoever owns the relationship
5. Confirm whether any fields require removal or aggregation before use

A. 3, 2, 4, 5, 1 · B. 3, 5, 2, 4, 1 · C. 3, 2, 5, 4, 1 · D. 3, 2, 5, 1, 4 · E. 2, 3, 5, 4, 1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C — 3, 2, 5, 4, 1.**

Recognise the governing instrument, read what it permits, apply whatever controls follow, obtain approval, then proceed. Activities 3, 2 and 5 are **Creation Diligence** — the judgement about what data you put into a system and on what basis. Activity 4, the written approval, is **Deployment Diligence**: someone with the authority accepting the use.

The trap this scenario is built around is that **there is no personal data**, which makes the whole thing feel low-risk. But the control attaches to the *agreement*, not to identifiers. Sensitivity is not only about personal data.

**D is the approval-as-formality trap.** Retrospective approval is not approval. If the relationship owner would have said no, the breach has already happened by the time they are asked.

Of the others, A seeks approval before establishing whether any fields need treatment, so the approval covers an incompletely specified plan; B applies controls before knowing which ones the agreement requires; E cannot be right, since you cannot read an agreement you have not realised applies.
</details>

**13.** `[prompt]` You are an associate who must draft the same kind of customer follow-up note several times a week, in a consistent house voice. You want to build a prompt you can reuse rather than starting fresh each time. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Run the prompt and compare what comes back against the criteria you established
2. Supply two short examples written in the house voice
3. State who the note is for and what it needs to achieve
4. Name the specific defect you found and revise the prompt accordingly
5. Set the required format, length and any hard constraints

A. 1, 3, 5, 2, 4 · B. 5, 3, 2, 1, 4 · C. 2, 3, 5, 1, 4 · D. 3, 5, 2, 4, 1 · E. 3, 5, 2, 1, 4

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E — 3, 5, 2, 1, 4.**

Steps 3, 5 and 2 are Description built up in Claude 101's order — setting the stage, then specifying the rules, then showing examples, which is one of the six documented prompting techniques and the reliable mechanism for voice consistency.

Steps 1 and 4 are the **Description–Discernment loop** closing: evaluate against criteria, then revise the description based on what you found. Note that this sequence ends on *revise* rather than on commit — iterative loops are cyclical, not terminal, which is the one documented exception to the usual prove-then-commit closing pair.

**D is the asserted-defect trap.** Reversing the final pair looks harmless and is not: it makes the revision a matter of taste rather than a response to a measured gap. Discernment supplies the finding; Refine acts on it.

Of the others, A opens by running and evaluating a prompt that has not been written yet; B specifies constraints before establishing the audience they serve; C opens with examples, before there is any statement of audience or purpose for them to exemplify.
</details>

**14.** `[workflow]` You are an associate asked to introduce Claude into your team's supplier onboarding process, which currently runs across nine manual steps and involves a compliance sign-off. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Roll the change out to the remaining teams
2. Classify each step by whether it suits Claude, needs judgement, or needs system access
3. Map the current nine steps from end to end
4. Pilot with one team and measure against the baseline
5. Design where human review sits in the new flow

A. 2, 3, 5, 4, 1 · B. 3, 2, 5, 4, 1 · C. 3, 2, 4, 5, 1 · D. 3, 2, 5, 1, 4 · E. 3, 5, 2, 4, 1

**15.** `[prevention]` You are an associate who has noticed that across many separate conversations you keep giving Claude the same correction — asking it to lead with the recommendation rather than the background. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Check a fresh conversation with the correction deliberately left out, to see whether the standing rule holds
2. Notice that you are giving the same correction repeatedly
3. Stop including the correction in individual prompts
4. Add the rule to your standing configuration
5. Work out what the underlying standing rule actually is

A. 5, 2, 4, 1, 3 · B. 2, 5, 3, 4, 1 · C. 2, 4, 5, 1, 3 · D. 2, 5, 4, 1, 3 · E. 2, 5, 4, 3, 1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D — 2, 5, 4, 1, 3.**

Notice the pattern, articulate the rule behind it, put it into configuration, verify it holds, then stop repeating it per conversation.

This encodes a documented practice: standing instructions are not expected to be complete on day one, and the corrections you find yourself giving over and over are the candidates for promotion into them. **A correction you keep repeating is a configuration candidate.**

It also rests on a fact from elsewhere in the framework: correcting the model teaches it nothing, so repeating a correction conversation by conversation is not a fix at any volume.

**E is the dropping-the-fallback-early trap.** This ordering abandons the manual correction across all your work and only then goes looking for evidence that the configuration replaced it. The right move is narrower: leave the correction out of one conversation, see what happens, and only then stop supplying it everywhere.

Of the others, A derives the standing rule before noticing the pattern it generalises; B stops correcting before the rule has been added to configuration at all; C configures the rule before articulating what the rule is.
</details>

**16.** `[eval]` You are an associate who has received a Claude-produced analysis of regional sales performance that you intend to use in a quarterly review pack. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Decide whether the analysis is fit to go into the pack
2. Check the specifics — the figures, the named accounts, the cited sources
3. Check whether anything the analysis was asked to cover is missing
4. Establish what the analysis needed to cover to be useful
5. Check whether the reasoning from the figures to the conclusions holds

A. 4, 2, 3, 5, 1 · B. 4, 5, 2, 3, 1 · C. 4, 2, 3, 1, 5 · D. 4, 2, 5, 1, 3 · E. 2, 4, 3, 5, 1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A — 4, 2, 3, 5, 1.**

Criteria, then specifics, then completeness, then reasoning, then the fitness judgement. Steps 2 and 3 are **Product Discernment** — accuracy and completeness, which the objectives treat as separate criteria for good reason, since a silent omission gives the reader no signal that anything is missing. Step 5 is **Process Discernment**, assessing how the output was arrived at rather than what it says.

Specifics are checked early because **fabrication clusters wherever a claim gets specific** — a name, a date, a figure, a citation. Precision is the signal to check rather than a sign of reliability, which makes it the highest-yield check to run first.

Accuracy and completeness are co-equal criteria and either could in principle be checked first. Hold the convention that puts accuracy first, because it is the order the objectives themselves state.

**C is the early-verdict trap.** Product Discernment is not the whole of Discernment. An analysis whose figures all check out can still reach a conclusion its figures do not support, and that is exactly the failure a review pack carries forward.

Of the others, B puts reasoning before the facts it operates on; D reaches the fitness verdict before checking whether anything the analysis was asked to cover is missing, so the verdict rests on an incomplete picture; E checks specifics before establishing what the analysis was supposed to cover, leaving nothing to assess completeness against.
</details>

**17.** `[approval]` You are an associate who has built an interactive summary as an artifact and wants to share it with a partner organisation outside your own. The conversation that produced it also had internal planning documents attached. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Publish and send the link
2. Confirm the content is accurate and appropriate for an external audience
3. Create the artifact
4. Check what files and conversation context the sharing method exposes
5. Get sign-off from whoever owns the external relationship

A. 3, 2, 4, 1, 5 · B. 3, 2, 5, 4, 1 · C. 3, 2, 4, 5, 1 · D. 2, 3, 4, 5, 1 · E. 3, 5, 2, 4, 1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C — 3, 2, 4, 5, 1.**

Create, check the content, check the exposure, obtain sign-off, publish. Steps 4, 5 and 1 are **Deployment Diligence** — and step 4 is the one candidates skip, which is why the stem mentions the attached internal documents.

Publishing makes an artifact reachable by anyone holding the link, and sharing can carry more than the artifact itself. Checking the exposure is a distinct act from checking the content, and it has to happen before anyone is asked to approve.

**B is the premature-approval trap.** Sign-off is being given on the content alone while the exposure question is still open. What the owner is actually accountable for is what reaches the partner, which is not yet known at that point.

Of the others, A publishes the link and obtains sign-off afterwards; D attempts to check the content of an artifact that has not been created; E obtains sign-off before either check has been made, so the relationship owner is approving an artefact nobody has examined.
</details>

**18.** `[handover]` You are an associate who built a Claude-assisted reporting workflow that has run well for two months. The workflow feeds a customer-facing report and carries two human review checkpoints. You are moving to another team and a colleague will take it over. Which sequence correctly orders these five activities?

1. Hand over day-to-day ownership of the workflow
2. Document where human review is required and what each check is meant to catch
3. Confirm your colleague can run it and gets the result they expect
4. Give your colleague access to the Project and its configuration
5. Document the known limitations and the conditions under which it should not be used

A. 4, 2, 5, 3, 1 · B. 2, 5, 4, 3, 1 · C. 2, 5, 3, 4, 1 · D. 3, 2, 5, 4, 1 · E. 2, 5, 4, 1, 3

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B — 2, 5, 4, 3, 1.**

Document the review points, document the limitations, grant access, verify they can run it, then transfer ownership.

Steps 2 and 5 are the essentials of a handover under the objective on communicating Claude's value **and its limitations** to stakeholders — and note what is *not* on the list: conversation transcripts, tier comparisons, token costs. Those are development detail. What the receiving colleague needs is where the human checkpoints sit and when not to trust the thing.

Documentation precedes access so the first thing your colleague meets is a documented workflow rather than an undocumented one. Verification precedes transfer because ownership should not move until it is known to work in someone else's hands.

**E is the handover-before-verification trap.** Ownership is the irreversible step. Transferring before the colleague has run it once successfully means any problem discovered afterwards belongs to someone who has never seen it work.

Of the others, A grants access before the review checkpoints are written down, so the colleague could run a customer-facing workflow without knowing where the human checks sit; C verifies your colleague can run it before granting the access needed to run it; D asks the colleague to confirm they can run it before they have either the documentation or access to the Project.
</details>
